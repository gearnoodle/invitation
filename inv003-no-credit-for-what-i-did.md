# INV003: No credit for what I did?

> Published on Nov 24, 2026

The second proposition says no credit. That one stings. If I worked hard on something, isn't it mine?

## 1. The objection

Blame and credit come as a pair. If nothing bad is anyone's fault, because everything has causes, then nothing good is anyone's achievement either. Not your degree, not your garden, not the song you wrote.

That's a real loss, and it's fair to feel it.

## 2. What you did grew from others

Think about anything you're proud of. You did it with a language someone taught you, a body someone fed, skills someone showed you, tools someone built, and a bit of luck. Even the will to try came from somewhere.

Newton said he saw further by "standing on the shoulders of giants." So does everyone. Most of the giants have no names.

## 3. AI makes it obvious

AI shows this more clearly than anything before it. An AI learns from millions of books, songs, pictures, articles and conversations that people made. When you ask it for something, it gives back a condensed mix of all that human work.

So when someone uses AI to write a text or make a picture, and takes the credit, whose work is it really? Mostly everyone's. Humanity's combined effort, squeezed into one answer. Even this blog was shaped with help from an AI.

In the old world, this made people angry, and with good reason. Their work was used without asking, and it threatened how they earned a living. Those are real harms. But they're about money and permission, and in a world where everyone has enough and nothing is owed back, they lose most of their bite.

What's left is the plain truth AI made visible: almost nothing anyone makes is theirs alone. It never was.

## 4. Praise the work and the effort, not the person

Praise says "you're great." That's a judgement, and it turns people into scores.

Praising a person is even a little dangerous. People start chasing praise and fearing its loss. Those who get it rise, and those who don't feel smaller. Studies of children found that praising them for being clever made them avoid hard tasks, so they wouldn't lose the label.

So praise anything except the person. "This soup is wonderful." "That idea really helped." "The bench looks beautiful." Talk about the thing and what it did, not about who's better.

Noticing effort is fine too. "You really kept at that" tells someone you saw what they went through. The same studies found that children praised for effort took on harder tasks and kept going. Just keep it as noticing, not ranking. Effort grew from others too, and "try harder" was the old world's favourite pressure.

And if someone praises you? Take it the other way, as thanks for what you did. You can gently say so: "Thanks, I'm glad the soup turned out well," or "Thanks, lots of people helped with that." No need to correct anyone. Just turn the praise toward the thing.

Thanks says "that helped." That's just telling you what your action did. Thanks stays. It's a gift, and like every gift here, nothing is owed back.

One exception: "I'm glad you're here" isn't praise. It's love, and love isn't a score.

## 5. It's still yours to live

The joy of making something, the pride of seeing it work, the meaning it gave you: none of that is taken away. Nobody owns what you did, but you lived it.

Credit is a score. Joy isn't.

## 6. Why let go of credit

Credit is quietly expensive. People fight over who invented what. They hide ideas so nobody else gets the credit. They rank each other, and the ranking becomes power.

Without credit, ideas flow. Anyone can build on anything, and nobody has to win.

## 7. Names can still stay

You can still say who did what. Not as ownership, just as information and kindness: "Aino fixed this, ask her how." If someone asks to be named, naming them is polite. It just isn't owed.

## In short

You don't own what you did. But you did it, you felt it, and others can be thankful for it. Preferably not for you, only for what you did. That's plenty.
