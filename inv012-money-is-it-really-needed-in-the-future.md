# INV012: Money. Is it really needed in the future?

Money does two jobs at once. It's a **signal**: it shows what is needed. And it's a **push**: it gets people to go and do it. If one day nothing is owed back, what takes over those two jobs? And how do we get from here to there?

## 1. Money as a signal

When bread runs short, its price goes up. Bakers notice and bake more. That's supply and demand, and nobody had to plan it. Millions of prices quietly tell millions of people what is wanted, and where.

That's clever. But the signal is bent. Demand only counts people who can pay. A hungry person with no money sends no signal at all, while a rich person's wish for a third home comes through loud and clear. So the world grows enough food for everyone, and some people still go hungry.

## 2. Money as a push

Most people go to work because they have to. No wage, no rent, no food. That push gets a lot done, including the drains, the night shifts and the jobs nobody would choose.

But it's a threat in disguise: work, or fall. And it pushes just as hard toward things that harm, as long as they pay. Things made to break. Adverts. Selling people what they don't need.

No blame here. Money is a tool people built for a world that never had enough. It did its job. The question is what comes next.

## 3. What could take over the signal

The signal doesn't need money. It needs the need to be visible.

The [Integral project](https://integralcollective.io) has a lot to say here. Its main point: a price squeezes many things into one number, and most of them get lost on the way. Instead, measure those things directly, and let everyone see them.

- **Show supply and demand directly.** What's needed next to what's there, without a price in between.
- **Count the need, not the wallet.** A hungry person counts as much as anyone, money or not.
- **Many signals, not one number.** How many hours something takes, which skills, which materials, how much energy, how long it lasts, how easy it is to repair, and what it does to the Earth. A price hides all of that. Here it's in the open.
- **An open list of what's needed.** From a blocked drain to a town short of nurses, out where anyone can see it, add to it or question it. Like an old village notebook, just bigger.
- **Shared designs.** Every design for a tool, a house or a machine is free to copy and improve. When one town finds a way to make something easier, every town gets it at once, and the need for effort drops everywhere.
- **Watch what really happens.** Does the thing break earlier than expected? Is the river getting strained? Is one kind of work always short of hands? The signal keeps checking reality and warns early, before a shortage becomes a crisis.
- **It informs, it doesn't order.** The signal never tells anyone what to do. People look at it and decide together, close to home.

## 4. What could take over the push

Take the push away, and many fear nothing gets done. INV002 and INV011 say otherwise. Most people give anyway: because it matters, because it's interesting, because someone needs it.

What's left is the work nobody wants. For that: make it better, let machines do it, split it into small pieces so nobody is stuck with it for life, and share it thinly across many.

The floor matters most here. When your food and home don't depend on a job, nobody needs pushing. A pull is enough.

## 5. Money that fades

We won't jump from here to there. Money will be around for a long time, and that's okay. The real question is: what kind of money helps us walk away from money?

The Integral project has another idea worth borrowing: time credits. They're not like money:

- **They can't be traded.** You can't sell them or give them away, so nobody can buy power with them.
- **They slowly fade.** Credits you don't use melt away, so there's nothing to pile up or pass on.
- **They measure real effort.** Hours of needed work, not bargaining.
- **They're meant to shrink.** As machines and shared designs make something easier, it takes fewer credits, until it takes none and leaves the system for good.

That last one is the key. A good currency for the way across is one designed to make itself unneeded.

This design would add one firm line: **the floor never costs credits.** Food, a home and care stay unconditional. Credits only help share what's still scarce above the floor, for as long as it's scarce. Otherwise a gift turns back into a debt, which is exactly what we're trying to leave behind.

## 6. One need at a time

Nobody has to ban money. It can be made unneeded, one thing at a time, easiest first:

- **Knowledge.** Copying costs nothing, and much of it is already free.
- **Energy, things, food, getting around, care.** Each one that no longer needs money is one less reason to take a bad job.
- **Homes, last.** The hardest, because so many savings are tied up in them.

The order matters. The floor has to come before the old economy shrinks. Pull money away before people feel safe, and they hold on to it tighter.

One day we might simply notice we haven't paid for anything in a while.

## 7. Open questions

- **Isn't counting hours just keeping score?** A little, yes. That's why the credits fade, never touch the floor, and are meant to disappear.
- **Should some hours count for more?** Integral counts an hour of rare or urgent work, like fine soldering during a shortage, as more than an ordinary hour. That sounds fair. But it could slowly turn into a new ranking of people, the kind no credit warns about. And who would decide how much more each kind of hour counts?
- **What about trade between places still using money?** Money will cross that border for a long time. How to keep it from leaking back in is an open question.

Better ideas are welcome.

## In short

Money is a signal and a push. The signal can be replaced by making the need visible. The push can be replaced by a floor under everyone and the pull of things worth doing. On the way, money that can't be hoarded, and fades as things get easier, can help. The best currency for the transition is one designed to disappear.
